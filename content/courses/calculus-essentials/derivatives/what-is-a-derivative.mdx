<Step kind="see" title="Zoom in until it's straight">

A car's speedometer reads 62 km/h. But speed is distance divided by time, and
at a single instant no time passes and no distance is covered. So what is the
speedometer measuring?

You already know how to measure change over an **interval**. Between $x$ and
$x + h$, a function changes at an average rate of

$$
\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

which is the slope of the straight line through those two points on its graph
(the _secant line_). The speedometer question is: what happens to that slope
when the interval shrinks to almost nothing?

Try it. This is $f(x) = x^2$ around the point $x = 1$. The amber line is the
secant across the window you can see. Zoom in and watch its slope.

<Interactive id="tangent-zoom" props={{ fn: "x^2", x0: 1 }} />

Two things happen as you zoom:

1. **The curve straightens out.** Zoomed in far enough, you cannot tell it
   apart from a straight line. That line is the **tangent line** at the point.
2. **The slope settles down.** It reads $3$, then $2.1$, then $2.01$, then
   $2.001$… It is closing in on $2$ and nothing else.

That number is the **derivative** of $f$ at $x = 1$.

<Callout kind="definition" title="The derivative">

The derivative of $f$ at $x$ is the limit of the average rate of change as the
interval shrinks:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

It is the slope of the tangent line at $x$, and the **instantaneous rate of
change** of $f$ there.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="insight" title="We never divide by zero">

Setting $h = 0$ directly gives $\frac{0}{0}$, which is meaningless. The limit
asks a different question: what value do the slopes _approach_ as $h$ gets
small? That question has a perfectly good answer.

</Callout>

You don't need to zoom every time. For $f(x) = x^2$ the algebra gives the
slope at **every** point at once.

<WorkedExample title="The derivative of x² from the definition">

Start with the change in $f$ between $x$ and $x + h$:

$$
f(x+h) - f(x) = (x+h)^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2
$$

Divide by $h$ to get the average rate of change. Since $h \neq 0$, it cancels:

$$
\frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h
$$

Now let $h$ shrink towards $0$. The $h$ fades away and $2x$ remains:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \,(2x + h) = 2x
$$

At $x = 1$ that gives $f'(1) = 2$ — exactly what the zoom showed. Notice the
$2x + h$ in the middle too: with $x = 1$ and $h = 0.1$ it predicts the slope
$2.1$ you saw on the way.

</WorkedExample>

</Step>

Because a curve looks like its tangent line up close, the tangent is a good
stand-in for the curve near the point. That gives you a way to predict nearby
values:

$$
f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x
$$

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

The speedometer finally makes sense: it shows the derivative of the car's
position with respect to time. That idea, **velocity is the derivative of
position**, is where physics picks calculus up.

The prediction formula you just used is more powerful than it looks. Apply it
again and again in small steps and you can follow a rocket, a planet or a
bouncing ball through time, even when no formula exists for where it will be.
That is Euler's method, and it is how simulations work.
