<Layer depth="intuition">

Imagine a cannon on a tower so tall that it pokes out above the air, 400
kilometres up. It fires sideways. How fast does the cannonball have to go so
that it never lands?

Find out. Start slow, and keep adding speed.

<Interactive id="orbit-cannon" props={{ speed: 4, goal: true }} />

A slow shot curves down and hits the ground. A faster one lands further away,
because the Earth is round and the ground curves away beneath it as it falls.
Fire it fast enough and the ground curves away _as fast as the ball falls
towards it_. The ball keeps falling and keeps missing. It is in orbit.

That is all an orbit is. Isaac Newton drew this very picture in the 1680s, and
it is still the best explanation there is.

So here is the surprise about rockets. Space is close: the edge of it is only
about 100 km above your head, nearer than the next big city. If reaching orbit
were about height, a rocket could go straight up and be done in a few minutes.
It would then fall straight back down, as the cannonball does when you set
its speed to zero.

Getting to orbit is about **speed**, not height. A rocket has to end up
travelling sideways at nearly 8 kilometres every second: about 28 000 km/h,
more than twenty times the speed of sound. That is where almost all the fuel
goes.

<Callout kind="insight" title="Why rockets tip over">

Watch a launch and the rocket starts leaning within seconds of leaving the
pad. It is not falling over. It goes up just far enough to get out of the
thick lower air, and from then on it spends its effort building sideways
speed.

</Callout>

</Layer>

<Layer depth="mechanism">

**Astronauts are not beyond gravity.** At the height of the Space Station,
gravity is still about 89% as strong as on the ground. If it suddenly
switched off, the station would fly away in a straight line. Gravity is
exactly what bends its path into a circle.

Astronauts float because they are **falling**, together with their
spacecraft, all the time. An orbit is free fall that never ends.

**Falling and missing, with numbers.** In one second, an object 400 km up
falls about 4.3 metres. In that same second, something moving sideways at
7.67 km/s travels far enough that the curved surface of the Earth has dropped
away beneath it by, again, 4.3 metres. So after one second it is exactly as
high as it was. And after the next second, and the next.

Slower than that, it falls more than the ground drops away, and loses height.
Faster, and it gains height for a while, tracing out a long oval.

<Question id="orbit.straight-up" />

**Why rockets are mostly fuel.** A rocket moves by throwing mass out of the
back, fast. By Newton's third law the exhaust pushes the rocket forwards. But
the fuel for the end of the journey has to be carried, and accelerated,
through the whole beginning of it. That fuel needs more fuel to lift it, which
needs more fuel still.

The result is brutal: to reach orbital speed with ordinary chemical engines,
well over 90% of what sits on the launch pad has to be propellant. The
structure, the engines and the payload share what is left.

**Why rockets come in stages.** Once a tank is empty it is dead weight, and
every kilogram of dead weight costs fuel to speed up. So rockets throw their
empty tanks away as they go. A two-stage rocket is two rockets stacked: the
first does the heavy lifting through the atmosphere and falls away, and the
much smaller second stage finishes the job of reaching orbital speed without
dragging the first along.

</Layer>

<Layer depth="maths">

**How fast is orbital speed?** An object moving in a circle of radius $r$ at
speed $v$ is accelerating towards the centre at $\dfrac{v^2}{r}$. For a
satellite, gravity is the only force, so that acceleration _is_ the local
strength of gravity, $\dfrac{GM}{r^2}$:

$$
\frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{GM}{r^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
$$

The satellite's own mass has cancelled, just as it does for a falling hammer.
For the Earth, $GM = 398\,600\ \text{km}^3/\text{s}^2$, and its radius is
6371 km. At an altitude of 400 km, $r = 6771$ km:

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{398\,600}{6771}} \approx 7.67\ \text{km/s}
$$

One lap is a circle of circumference $2\pi r$, so it takes

$$
T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi \times 6771}{7.67} \approx 5545\ \text{s} \approx 92\ \text{minutes}
$$

Astronauts on the station see sixteen sunrises a day.

<Question id="orbit.period" />

**Height or speed: which costs more?** Compare the energy each kilogram needs.

- To be lifted 400 km against gravity: about 3.7 million joules.
- To be moving at 7.67 km/s: $\tfrac{1}{2}v^2 \approx 29$ million joules.

Getting up there is about an eighth of the cost of staying up there.

**The rocket equation.** A rocket that starts with mass $m_0$ and ends, tanks
empty, with mass $m_1$, having thrown its exhaust backwards at speed $v_e$,
changes its own speed by

$$
\Delta v = v_e \,\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m_1}
$$

This is Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation. The logarithm is the cruel part.
Reaching orbit takes a $\Delta v$ of about 9.4 km/s (orbital speed, plus what
is lost to gravity and air on the way up). A good kerosene engine has
$v_e \approx 3$ km/s. Solving for the mass ratio:

$$
\frac{m_0}{m_1} = e^{\Delta v / v_e} = e^{9.4/3} \approx 23
$$

The rocket must be 23 times heavier at lift-off than at burnout: about 96%
propellant. With stages, each one only has to manage a part of the
$\Delta v$, with a much kinder mass ratio.
