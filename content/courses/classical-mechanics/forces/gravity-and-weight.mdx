<Step kind="see" title="Why don't heavy things fall faster?">

then try it.

They land together. The hammer is more than forty times heavier, so gravity
really does pull on it more than forty times as hard. And yet
it falls no faster. You now have the tools to see exactly why.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Weight">

An object's **weight** is the force of gravity on it. Near the surface of a
planet it is

$$
W = mg
$$

pointing straight down, where $g$ is the strength of gravity there: about
9.8 N for every kilogram on Earth.

</Callout>

Now put the weight into Newton's second law. For an object with nothing else
acting on it, the net force _is_ its weight:

$$
a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{mg}{m} = g
$$

The mass cancels. A heavier object is pulled harder, in exact proportion to
its mass. But it is also harder to accelerate, in exact proportion to its
mass. The two effects cancel perfectly, every time, for every object.

That is why "$g$" appears with two different units that turn out to be the
same thing: 9.8 newtons per kilogram, the pull on each kilogram, and
9.8 m/s², the acceleration of anything in free fall.

<Callout kind="insight" title="Mass is yours; weight depends on where you are">

**Mass** measures how much matter an object has and how hard it is to
accelerate. It is the same everywhere. **Weight** is a force, and it changes
with the local gravity.

| World   | $g$ (m/s²) |
| ------- | ---------- |
| Earth   | 9.8        |
| Moon    | 1.6        |
| Mars    | 3.7        |
| Jupiter | 24.8       |

A 70 kg astronaut weighs about 690 N on Earth and about 110 N on the Moon.
She is still 70 kg, and just as hard to push sideways.

</Callout>

So what do bathroom scales measure? Not your weight directly. They measure how
hard they are pushing up on you: the normal force. Standing still, that equals
your weight. But take the scales into a lift whose cable has snapped, and both
you and the scales fall together with acceleration $g$. Nothing needs to hold
you up, so the scales read zero.

That is what "weightless" means. Astronauts in orbit are not beyond the reach
of gravity. At the height of the International Space Station, gravity is still
about 90% as strong as on the ground. They float because they, and the station
around them, are in continuous free fall.

</Step>
