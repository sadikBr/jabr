Close to the point where it touches, a tangent line is almost
indistinguishable from the curve. That makes it useful: a straight line is
easy to calculate with, even when the curve is not.

Suppose you need $\sqrt{4.1}$ and have no calculator. You know $\sqrt{4} = 2$
exactly, and the slope of $\sqrt{x}$ at $x = 4$ is $\tfrac{1}{4}$. Follow the
tangent a distance of $0.1$:

$$
\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \times 0.1 = 2.025
$$

The true value is $2.02485\ldots$, so the estimate is off by less than two
parts in ten thousand. This is called a **linear approximation**, and
engineers lean on it constantly.

### How far can you trust it?

The further you go from the point of contact, the more the curve has bent
away from the line. The interesting question is _how quickly_ the error
grows. Your program will measure it.

### Your task

Complete `estimate` and `error`, using the `tangent` function from the last
milestone. The program then prints a table for $\sqrt{x}$ near $x = 4$.
Compare the errors at distances 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4: what happens to the error
each time the distance doubles?
