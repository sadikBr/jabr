<Step kind="see" title="Rowing across a river">

You want to row straight across a river to a jetty on the far bank. You can
row at 3 m/s. The river flows past at 2 m/s. If you point the boat straight at
the jetty and row, where do you end up?

Not at the jetty. While you row across, the water carries you downstream. Your
actual motion is a combination of the two, and "3 m/s" plus "2 m/s" is not
5 m/s, because the two are in different directions.

Below, the purple arrow is your rowing and the red arrow is the current. The
amber arrow is where you really go. Change the direction you row in until you
travel straight across.

You had to aim upstream, at about 132°, so that part of your rowing cancels
the current. And notice your speed over the ground once you do: not 3 m/s,
but about 2.2 m/s. Some of your effort is spent just standing still against
the river.

Velocity is an example of a quantity where direction matters as much as size.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Vectors and scalars">

A **vector** has a size (its _magnitude_) and a direction. Displacement,
velocity, acceleration and force are all vectors. We draw them as arrows.

A **scalar** has a size only: mass, time, temperature, speed.

To add two vectors, put them **tip to tail**: start the second arrow where the
first one ends. Their sum runs from the very start to the very end. The order
does not matter.

</Callout>

Drawing arrows is fine for building intuition, but nobody wants to calculate
with a ruler and protractor. The practical way to handle vectors is to split
each one into two parts at right angles, one horizontal and one vertical.

In the next workbench there is a single vector. Change its size and direction
and watch its two parts, drawn dashed. Then try to hit the target.

A horizontal part of 3 and a vertical part of −4 needed a vector of size
exactly 5. That is Pythagoras: the two parts and the vector make a
right-angled triangle, and $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Components">

A vector of magnitude $A$ at an angle $\theta$, measured anticlockwise from the
positive $x$ direction, has **components**

$$
A_x = A\cos\theta \qquad\qquad A_y = A\sin\theta
$$

Going the other way, from components back to size and direction:

$$
A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2} \qquad\qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}
$$

</Callout>

<Callout kind="insight" title="To add vectors, add their components">

Tip-to-tail addition becomes ordinary arithmetic, one direction at a time:

$$
R_x = A_x + B_x \qquad\qquad R_y = A_y + B_y
$$

This is the whole reason components are worth the trouble. Two dimensions
turn into two separate one-dimensional problems.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="The boat, by components">

Take $x$ as downstream and $y$ as straight across. The current is
$(2,\ 0)$ m/s. You row at 3 m/s, at an angle to be found. To travel straight
across, the $x$ components must cancel:

$$
3\cos\theta + 2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \cos\theta = -\tfrac{2}{3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta \approx 131.8°
$$

Then the $y$ component of your rowing is all that is left:

$$
3\sin(131.8°) \approx 2.24 \text{ m/s}
$$

That is the speed at which you cross, and it matches what the workbench
showed. (By Pythagoras it is $\sqrt{3^2 - 2^2} = \sqrt{5}$.)

</WorkedExample>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

Components are about to earn their keep. Throw a ball at an angle, and its
velocity has a horizontal part and a vertical part. Gravity only acts on the
vertical one. Treat them separately and a complicated-looking curved path
turns into two simple motions you already understand.

Forces are vectors too. When several act on one object, they add tip to tail
just like the rowing and the current, and it is the sum, the **net force**,
that decides what the object does.
