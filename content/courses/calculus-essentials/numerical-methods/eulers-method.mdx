<Step kind="see" title="Follow the slope">

So far you have started with a function and found its derivative. The world
usually sets the opposite problem: you know how fast something is _changing_,
and you want to know what it will _be_.

A colony of bacteria is a good example. The more bacteria there are, the
faster the colony grows. In the simplest version, its rate of growth equals
its size:

$$
\frac{dy}{dt} = y
$$

Suppose the colony starts at $y = 1$ (thousand, say). What is $y$ two hours
later? Nobody has handed you a formula for $y$. All you have is a rule for its
slope.

But you already own a tool that turns a slope into a prediction. Near any
point, a curve is close to its tangent line:

$$
y(t + \Delta t) \approx y(t) + y'(t)\,\Delta t
$$

So take a small step along the tangent. You land at a new point, where the
rule gives you a new slope. Step again. And again.

That is the whole method. Below, the purple curve is the true answer and the
amber path is what stepping along tangents gives. Start with a few big steps,
then add more.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Euler's method">

To follow $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = f(t, y)$ from a known starting point $(t_0, y_0)$,
choose a step size $\Delta t$ and repeat:

$$
y_{n+1} = y_n + f(t_n, y_n)\,\Delta t \qquad\qquad t_{n+1} = t_n + \Delta t
$$

In words: new value = old value + slope × step.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Four steps by hand">

Take $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = y$ from $y(0) = 1$ to $t = 2$ in four steps, so
$\Delta t = 0.5$. Here the slope is simply the current value of $y$.

| Step | $t$   | $y$     | Slope   | $y + \text{slope} \times 0.5$ |
| ---- | ----- | ------- | ------- | ----------------------------- |
| 1    | $0$   | $1$     | $1$     | $1.5$                         |
| 2    | $0.5$ | $1.5$   | $1.5$   | $2.25$                        |
| 3    | $1$   | $2.25$  | $2.25$  | $3.375$                       |
| 4    | $1.5$ | $3.375$ | $3.375$ | $5.0625$                      |

Euler's method says $y(2) \approx 5.06$. The true answer is $e^2 \approx 7.39$,
so four steps leave us short by about $2.3$.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="insight" title="Why it falls behind, and how to fix it">

Each step uses the slope at the _start_ of the step and pretends it stays the
same until the end. But the colony keeps growing during the step, so the true
slope keeps rising, and the straight-line prediction falls behind.

Smaller steps give the slope less time to go stale. In the explorer, 16 steps
were off by about $0.8$, 32 steps by $0.43$ and 64 steps by $0.22$. Each time
you halve the step size, you roughly halve the error.

</Callout>

A cup of coffee cools quickly at first and then more slowly, because the rate
of cooling depends on how much hotter it is than the room. For a room at
20 °C, with time in minutes:

$$
\frac{dT}{dt} = -0.5\,(T - 20)
$$

One giant step sends the coffee to $-190$ °C. Steps that are too big do not
just lose accuracy: they can produce nonsense. Every simulation has to choose
a step small enough to stay stable.

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

You now have the engine of every physics simulation. A game or a flight
simulator keeps track of where each object is and how fast it is moving. Many
times a second it works out the forces, turns them into an acceleration, and
takes one Euler step:

$$
v \leftarrow v + a\,\Delta t \qquad\qquad x \leftarrow x + v\,\Delta t
$$

Nothing else is needed. No formula for the path has to exist, which is just as
well, because once air drag or a burning rocket engine is involved, none does.

Time to build one.
