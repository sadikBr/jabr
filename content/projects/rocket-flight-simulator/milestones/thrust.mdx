A thrown ball only ever slows down on the way up. A rocket carries its own
push. For this milestone it flies straight up, so you are back to one
dimension, with two new ideas.

### Thrust

A rocket engine works by throwing mass out of the back, fast. The force it
gets in return is

$$
T = \dot{m}\, v_e
$$

where $\dot{m}$ is how many kilograms of fuel it burns each second and $v_e$
is the speed the exhaust leaves at. A small model rocket burning
$0.1\ \text{kg/s}$ with an exhaust speed of $300\ \text{m/s}$ gets
$30\ \text{N}$ of thrust.

### A changing mass

Newton's second law gives the acceleration from the net force:

$$
a = \frac{T}{m} - g
$$

But $m$ is not constant: the rocket is burning its fuel. As it gets lighter,
the same thrust accelerates it harder. There is no need for anything clever
here. The loop just recomputes the mass at every step.

When the fuel runs out, the thrust drops to zero and the rocket coasts,
slowing under gravity until it falls back.

<Callout kind="insight" title="The rocket equation">

For this setup there is an exact answer for the speed at burnout, found by
Konstantin Tsiolkovsky in 1903:

$$
v = v_e \ln\!\frac{m_0}{m_f} - g\,t_b
$$

where $m_0$ is the mass at lift-off, $m_f$ the mass once the fuel is gone and
$t_b$ the burn time. The logarithm is why real rockets are mostly fuel: to go
faster you need the ratio $m_0 / m_f$ to grow, and it grows slowly.

</Callout>
