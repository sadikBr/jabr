<Step kind="see" title="How far does it take to stop?">

You are driving along a country road when a tree comes down ahead of you. Two
things happen before you stop. First, for about a second, nothing: that is how
long it takes you to notice and get your foot to the brake. Then the car slows
down at a steady rate until it is at rest.

How fast can you be going and still stop in time? Make a guess, then find out.

Try 40 km/h and then 80 km/h, and compare the two distances underneath.
Doubling the speed doubles the **thinking distance**, as you would expect. But
it makes the **braking distance** four times as long. To see why, you need to
describe the motion precisely.

While the driver reacts, the velocity is constant, and distance is simply
velocity × time. While the car brakes, the _acceleration_ is constant. That is
the next simplest kind of motion, and it has its own small set of equations.

On a velocity–time graph, constant acceleration is a straight line. If the
velocity starts at $u$ and changes at a steady rate $a$, then after a time $t$
it is

$$
v = u + at
$$

The distance covered is the area under that line: you saw it shaded in the
graph above. The area of that shape is its average height times its width, and
the average of a straight line is halfway between its ends:

$$
s = \frac{u + v}{2}\,t = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2
$$

<Callout kind="definition" title="Equations for constant acceleration">

For motion in a straight line with constant acceleration $a$, starting at
velocity $u$:

$$
v = u + at \qquad\quad s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \qquad\quad v^2 = u^2 + 2as
$$

Here $v$ is the velocity after time $t$, and $s$ is the displacement in that
time. The third equation comes from the first two by eliminating $t$. It is
the one to reach for when time is not mentioned.

</Callout>

These agree with what you know from calculus. Differentiate
$s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ with respect to $t$ and you get $u + at$, the
velocity. Differentiate again and you get $a$.

<WorkedExample title="An emergency stop, in numbers">

A car is travelling at $20$ m/s (72 km/h). The driver takes $1$ s to react, and
then the brakes slow the car at $8$ m/s². How far does it travel before it
stops?

**Thinking.** Constant velocity for one second: $20 \times 1 = 20$ m.

**Braking.** We know $u = 20$, the final velocity $v = 0$, and $a = -8$
(negative, because the car is slowing down). Time is not mentioned, so use
$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$:

$$
0 = 20^2 + 2 \times (-8) \times s \quad\Rightarrow\quad s = \frac{400}{16} = 25 \text{ m}
$$

**Total.** $20 + 25 = 45$ m.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="insight" title="Why speed is so unforgiving">

Setting $v = 0$ in the third equation gives the braking distance for any
speed:

$$
s = \frac{u^2}{2 \times \text{deceleration}}
$$

The speed is _squared_. Go twice as fast and you need four times the distance.
Go three times as fast and you need nine.

</Callout>

One constant acceleration is more famous than all the others. Near the Earth's
surface, anything falling freely (with air resistance small enough to ignore)
speeds up at the same rate, whatever it is made of and however heavy it is.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Free fall">

An object moving under gravity alone is in **free fall**. Its acceleration is
$g \approx 9.8$ m/s², directed downwards.

That is true on the way up as well as on the way down. With upwards as
positive, a ball thrown upwards at speed $u$ has

$$
v = u - gt \qquad\qquad y = ut - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2
$$

</Callout>

Why $g$ is the same for a hammer and a feather is a question about forces, and
it gets its own lesson later in this course.

</Step>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

Everything here happened along a single straight line. Most real motion does
not: a ball thrown across a field moves sideways and up and down at the same
time. The trick is to treat each direction separately, and for that you need
**vectors**.

And there is a bigger question underneath all of this. These equations
_describe_ motion with constant acceleration. They do not say what causes an
acceleration, or how big it will be. That is the business of **forces**.
