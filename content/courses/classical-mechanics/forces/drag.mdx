<Step kind="see" title="Why raindrops don't hurt">

A raindrop falls from a cloud about two kilometres up. If gravity were the
only force on it, the equations of free fall say it would hit you at around
200 metres per second, faster than a racing car. Rain would be lethal.

Instead it arrives at a gentle 9 m/s or so. Something stops it speeding up,
and you already know what. Drop the hammer and the feather again, this time on
Earth.

Anything moving through air has to shove air out of its way, and the air
pushes back. That push is called **drag**.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Air drag">

Drag acts in the direction opposite to the motion. For most everyday objects
moving at everyday speeds, its size grows with the **square** of the speed:

$$
F_{\text{drag}} = k\,v^2
$$

The constant $k$ depends on the object's size and shape and on the density of
the air: a parachute has a large $k$, a dart a small one. It does **not**
depend on the object's mass.

</Callout>

That last point explains the feather. Gravity pulls on each object in
proportion to its mass, but drag does not care about mass at all. For the
hammer, drag is tiny compared with its weight and barely matters. For the
feather, drag is comparable to its weight almost immediately.

Now follow a falling object from the moment it is released, using its
free-body diagram: weight $mg$ downwards, drag $kv^2$ upwards.

- **At first** it is moving slowly, so the drag is tiny. The net force is
  nearly the whole weight and it accelerates at nearly $g$.
- **As it speeds up** the drag grows. The net force shrinks, so it is still
  gaining speed but more and more slowly.
- **Eventually** the drag grows to equal the weight. The net force is zero, so
  the acceleration is zero, and the speed stops changing.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Terminal velocity">

The steady speed at which drag balances weight is the **terminal velocity**.
Setting $k v_t^2 = mg$:

$$
v_t = \sqrt{\frac{mg}{k}}
$$

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A skydiver, before and after">

A skydiver has a mass of 80 kg. Falling spread-eagled, $k \approx 0.25$ kg/m:

$$
v_t = \sqrt{\frac{80 \times 9.8}{0.25}} = \sqrt{3136} = 56 \text{ m/s}
$$

That is about 200 km/h. Opening the parachute raises $k$ to about 30 kg/m:

$$
v_t = \sqrt{\frac{80 \times 9.8}{30}} \approx 5.1 \text{ m/s}
$$

which is about the speed you reach by jumping off a wall a little over a
metre high. Survivable.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="insight" title="Why small things fall slowly">

Make an object twice as big in every direction and its mass goes up eight
times, but the area it presents to the air only four times. Weight wins, and
its terminal velocity rises. Shrink it and drag wins.

That is why dust floats, drizzle drifts, and raindrops fall faster than fog.
The biologist J. B. S. Haldane pointed out that a mouse can survive a fall
down a mine shaft that would kill a person.

</Callout>

There is a catch. With drag, the acceleration changes all the time, so the
constant-acceleration equations no longer apply. The speed follows
$\frac{dv}{dt} = g - \frac{k}{m}v^2$: a rule for the slope, not a formula for
the answer. That is a job for Euler's method.

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

With weight and drag you can now explain the whole of the hammer and feather
story, on the Moon and on Earth.

You also have every ingredient of a flight simulator. A rocket's free-body
diagram has three arrows: thrust, weight and drag. At each moment they give a
net force; the second law turns it into an acceleration; and one Euler step
turns that into a new velocity and a new position. None of the three forces
stays constant during a launch, and it does not matter. The loop does not
care.

That is the capstone project, and you are ready for it.
