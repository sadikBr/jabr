<Step kind="see" title="One object, every force">

The second law needs the **net** force on an object. Finding it is where most
mistakes in mechanics are made: a force gets forgotten, or one is invented
that is not really there.

The cure is a drawing. Pick one object, sketch it on its own, and draw an
arrow for every force acting **on it**. Nothing else goes in the picture. This
is called a **free-body diagram**.

Try it. For each situation, tick the forces you think belong, then check.
Watch out for the last option in the list.

<Callout kind="definition" title="How to draw a free-body diagram">

1. **Choose one object** and draw it alone.
2. **Gravity:** draw its weight, straight down. It is always there.
3. **Contact:** go round everything touching the object. A surface gives a
   push at right angles to itself (and friction along itself, if it is
   rough). A taut rope pulls along its length. Air or water pushes back
   against motion through it.
4. **Stop.** If nothing is touching it and it is not gravity, it is not a
   force on this object.

</Callout>

Two of those forces deserve a closer look.

<Callout kind="definition" title="The normal force">

A surface pushes back on anything pressed against it. This push is always
**at right angles to the surface** ("normal" is the mathematical word for
perpendicular), and it is exactly as big as it needs to be to stop the object
sinking in.

It is _not_ always equal to the object's weight. On a slope it is smaller. In
a lift that is speeding up, it is larger.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="definition" title="Net force">

The **net force** is the vector sum of every force in the diagram. It is the
only thing the second law cares about:

$$
\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \dots = m\,\vec{a}
$$

If the arrows cancel, the net force is zero and the velocity does not change.
If they do not, the object accelerates in the direction of what is left over.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning" title="There is no force of motion">

It is very tempting to draw an arrow in the direction something is moving,
"to keep it going". Do not. The first law says moving things keep moving on
their own. If you cannot name the object that is doing the pushing or
pulling, the force does not exist.

</Callout>

Once the diagram is right, the rest is vector addition. When forces point in
awkward directions, split them into components along two perpendicular axes,
and choose the axes to make life easy.

<WorkedExample title="A block on a smooth ramp">

A block of mass $m$ slides down a frictionless ramp tilted at an angle
$\theta$. What is its acceleration?

**The diagram.** Two forces: the weight $mg$, straight down, and the normal
force $N$, at right angles to the ramp.

**The axes.** The block can only move along the ramp, so take one axis along
the slope and one at right angles to it. The normal force already lies along
the second axis. Only the weight needs splitting:

- along the slope: $mg\sin\theta$
- into the slope: $mg\cos\theta$

**At right angles to the slope** the block does not accelerate, so the forces
balance: $N = mg\cos\theta$.

**Along the slope** only one component acts, so the second law gives

$$
mg\sin\theta = ma \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = g\sin\theta
$$

The mass has cancelled. On a 30° ramp, everything slides with an acceleration
of $9.8 \times \sin 30° = 4.9$ m/s², half of free fall. A flat ramp gives
zero, and a vertical one gives $g$, as they should.

</WorkedExample>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

You now have the complete method of classical mechanics:

1. draw the free-body diagram,
2. add the forces to get the net force,
3. divide by the mass to get the acceleration,
4. follow the motion from there.

The remaining lessons in this module look at two particular forces that turn
up in almost every diagram: the weight, and the drag of the air. Put those
two together with the thrust of an engine and you have every force on a
rocket.
