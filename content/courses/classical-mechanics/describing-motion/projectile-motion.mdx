<Step kind="see" title="Two motions at once">

Kick a ball, throw a stone, fire a cannon: once it leaves, the only thing
acting on it is gravity (as long as the air does not matter much). Anything
moving like that is called a **projectile**.

Start by getting a feel for it. Land the ball on the flag.

There is more than one way to do it. At 25 m/s, both 30° and 60° land in the
same place: one low and fast, one high and slow. To see why, split the motion
in two.

Gravity pulls straight down. It has no sideways part at all. So:

- **Horizontally**, nothing speeds the ball up or slows it down. It keeps the
  horizontal velocity it started with.
- **Vertically**, the ball is in free fall, exactly as if it had been thrown
  straight up.

The two do not affect each other. The curved path is just these two simple
motions happening at the same time.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Projectile motion">

A projectile launched at speed $v$ and angle $\theta$ above the horizontal
starts with velocity components

$$
v_x = v\cos\theta \qquad\qquad v_y = v\sin\theta
$$

After a time $t$ its position is

$$
x = v_x\,t \qquad\qquad y = v_y\,t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2
$$

Constant velocity sideways, constant acceleration up and down.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A launch at 20 m/s and 30°">

Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

**Split the velocity.** $v_x = 20\cos 30° \approx 17.3$ m/s and
$v_y = 20\sin 30° = 10$ m/s.

**Time in the air** depends only on the vertical motion. The ball is back on
the ground when $y = 0$:

$$
0 = 10t - 4.9t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \frac{10}{4.9} \approx 2.04 \text{ s}
$$

**How far it goes** is the horizontal velocity times that time:

$$
x = 17.3 \times 2.04 \approx 35.3 \text{ m}
$$

**How high it gets** is the vertical motion again: at the top $v_y$ has
fallen to zero, so $0 = 10^2 - 2 \times 9.8 \times h$, giving
$h \approx 5.1$ m.

</WorkedExample>

Doing those same steps with symbols instead of numbers gives two formulas
worth knowing for a launch from level ground:

$$
T = \frac{2v\sin\theta}{g} \qquad\qquad R = \frac{v^2\sin 2\theta}{g}
$$

<Callout kind="insight" title="Why 45° goes furthest, and why 30° matches 60°">

The range depends on the angle through $\sin 2\theta$. That is as large as it
can be, $1$, when $2\theta = 90°$: a launch at 45°.

And $\sin 2\theta$ takes the same value for two angles that add up to 90°,
because $\sin 60° = \sin 120°$. A steep launch stays up longer but moves
sideways more slowly, and the two effects balance exactly.

</Callout>

</Step>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

Everything on this page assumed no air. Turn on air resistance in the
launcher at the top and the ball falls short, the path is no longer a
parabola, and no tidy formula describes it. The only way to follow the motion
then is to step it forwards a moment at a time, which is what Euler's method
does and what you will build in the rocket project.

First, though, the missing piece. You now know how to _describe_ motion in
