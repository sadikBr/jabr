So far everything has moved along one line. Now launch something at an angle
and it moves in two directions at once. Try it:

<Interactive id="projectile-sim" props={{ speed: 20, angle: 30 }} />

Launch at 30°, then at 60°. They land in the same place. Then try 45°.

### Two motions, side by side

The trick to two dimensions is that the two directions do not interfere.
Split the launch velocity into a horizontal and a vertical part:

$$
v_x = v\cos\theta \qquad\qquad v_y = v\sin\theta
$$

- **Sideways** nothing pushes or pulls, so $v_x$ never changes. This is the
  constant-velocity point from the first milestone.
- **Vertically** gravity acts, so $v_y$ changes. This is the falling object
  from the second.

Run both at once and you get the curved path.

### Something to check against

With no air and flat ground, the distance to landing has a neat formula:

$$
R = \frac{v^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}
$$

It explains what you saw: $\sin(2\theta)$ is the same for 30° and 60°, and
largest at 45°. Your simulation will not use this formula at all. If the two
agree anyway, you can trust the simulation when there is no formula to check
against, which is exactly where the rocket is heading.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Radians">

Python's `math.sin` and `math.cos` expect radians. `math.radians(angle_deg)`
converts from degrees.

</Callout>
