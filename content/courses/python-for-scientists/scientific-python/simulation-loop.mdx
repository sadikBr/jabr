Everything in this course has been leading here. You have variables to hold

and plots to see it. Put them together and you can simulate.

This program drops a ball from 20 metres and follows it down in steps of a

<Callout kind="definition" title="The simulation loop">

1. **Set up the state:** the quantities that describe the system right now
   (here time `t`, height `y` and velocity `v`), and a small time step `dt`.
2. **Repeat:**
   - work out the **acceleration** from the forces,
   - use it to update the **velocity**,
   - use that to update the **position**,
   - move the **clock** on by `dt`,
   - **record** the new state.
3. **Stop** when something happens (the ball lands) or time runs out.

</Callout>

The two update lines are Euler's method from the calculus course, used twice:
new value = old value + rate of change × time step. Velocity is the rate of
change of position; acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

<Callout kind="insight" title="The physics lives in one line">

Look at the line `a = -g`. That is the only place the program says anything
about _what kind of world_ the ball is in. Replace it with a different rule
(add air drag, a spring, a rocket engine) and the same loop simulates
something else entirely. Nothing else needs to change.

</Callout>

The step `dt` is a trade. In the program above, try `dt = 0.5` and then
`dt = 0.01`, and compare the landing time with the exact answer, 2.02 s. A
smaller step follows the true motion more closely and takes more passes round
the loop to get there.

</Step>
