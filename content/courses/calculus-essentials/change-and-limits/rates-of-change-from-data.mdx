<Step kind="see" title="How fast is the fastest human?">

In Berlin in 2009, Usain Bolt set a 100 m world record of 9.58 seconds. So how
fast was he going?

The obvious answer is distance divided by time: $100 \div 9.58 \approx 10.4$
metres per second. But he started from a standstill, so he cannot have been
doing 10.4 m/s the whole way. For part of the race he must have been going
faster than that. How much faster?

The timing system recorded the moment he passed each 10 m mark, so we can find
out. Each dot below is one of those readings. Pick any two, and the line
through them shows his **average speed** between them: the distance covered
divided by the time taken.

You should have found it between 60 m and 70 m: ten metres in 0.81 seconds,
which is about 12.3 m/s, or 44 km/h. That is well above his "average" of 10.4.

What you just did has a name.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Average rate of change">

Between two inputs $a$ and $b$, the average rate of change of a function $f$ is

$$
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}
$$

the change in the output divided by the change in the input. On a graph it is
the slope of the straight line through the two points, called the **secant
line**.

</Callout>

When the input is time and the output is distance, the average rate of change
is an average speed. But the same calculation measures how fast _anything_
changes: litres per minute draining from a tank, degrees per hour as a room
warms up, euros per year in a savings account.

<WorkedExample title="A speed from two rows of a table">

Here are four of Bolt's readings.

| Distance (m) | 20   | 50   | 70   | 100  |
| ------------ | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| Time (s)     | 2.88 | 5.47 | 7.10 | 9.58 |

His average speed between the 20 m and 50 m marks was

$$
\frac{50 - 20}{5.47 - 2.88} = \frac{30}{2.59} \approx 11.6 \text{ m/s}
$$

Always subtract "later minus earlier" on both the top and the bottom, in the
same order. The units come along for free: metres divided by seconds gives
metres per second.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="insight" title="An average hides what happens in between">

The line across the whole race said 10.4 m/s. It told you nothing about the
slow start or the peak in the middle. Shorter intervals reveal more.

Keep one question in mind for later: what would the _shortest possible_
interval tell you?

</Callout>

</Step>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

On the ramp, the average speeds starting from $t = 2$ came out as 6, then 5,
then 4.5, then 4.1 as the interval shrank. They are clearly heading somewhere.

But you cannot simply shrink the interval to nothing: over zero seconds the
ball covers zero metres, and $0 \div 0$ is meaningless. Asking where a
sequence of values is _heading_, without ever arriving, is what **limits**
are for. With limits in hand, the speed at a single instant finally makes
sense. It is called the **derivative**.
