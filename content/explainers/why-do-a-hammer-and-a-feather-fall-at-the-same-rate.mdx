<Layer depth="intuition">

In 1971, standing on the Moon, astronaut David Scott held out a geology hammer
in one hand and a falcon feather in the other, and let go of both. They fell
side by side and hit the dust at the same moment.

On Earth the feather would have drifted down long after the hammer. The only
difference is **air**. The Moon has none, so nothing got in the feather's way.

But that still leaves a puzzle. The hammer was more than 40 times heavier. Gravity
really was pulling on it that much harder. So why didn't it win?

Because a heavy thing is also **harder to get moving**. The hammer gets 40
times the pull, but it takes 40 times the pull to speed it up by the same
amount. The two effects cancel exactly, every time, for every object.

<Callout kind="insight" title="Galileo's thought experiment">

Suppose heavy things did fall faster. Tie a light stone to a heavy one. The
slow light stone should hold the heavy one back, so the pair falls _slower_.
But the pair is also heavier than either stone, so it should fall _faster_.
Both cannot be true. The only way out: everything falls at the same rate.

</Callout>

</Layer>

<Layer depth="mechanism">

Two different properties of an object are in play, and both are measured by
its mass.

**How hard gravity pulls.** The pull of gravity on an object, its _weight_,
is proportional to its mass. Twice the mass, twice the weight.

**How hard it is to accelerate.** Newton's second law says that the
acceleration you get is the force divided by the mass. Twice the mass, half
the acceleration for the same force.

Put Scott's two objects through both steps, using the Moon's gravity:

|         | Mass    | Pull of gravity | Acceleration = pull ÷ mass |
| ------- | ------- | --------------- | -------------------------- |
| Hammer  | 1.32 kg | 2.14 N          | 1.62 m/s²                  |
| Feather | 0.03 kg | 0.049 N         | 1.62 m/s²                  |

The hammer feels a force 44 times larger and has 44 times as much mass to
move. The acceleration comes out identical. Both objects gain speed at the
same rate, so they stay side by side all the way down.

**So what does air change?** Air pushes back on anything moving through it.
That push, called _drag_, depends on the object's shape and speed, and not on
its mass. A feather is all surface and almost no weight, so a tiny amount of
drag is enough to balance its weight, and it stops speeding up almost
immediately. The hammer's weight is so much larger that the same drag barely
matters.

<Callout kind="note" title="Falling is not about weight">

Without air, a bowling ball, a feather and a grain of sand dropped together
land together. With air, what matters is weight _compared with_ drag.

</Callout>

</Layer>

<Layer depth="maths">

Call the object's mass $m$ and the strength of gravity $g$. Its weight is

$$
F = m g
$$

Newton's second law, $F = m a$, gives its acceleration:

$$
m a = m g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = g
$$

The mass cancels. That one line is the whole answer: the acceleration of a
falling object does not depend on what it is.

**How long did the Moon drop take?** Starting from rest and falling a height
$h$ with constant acceleration $g$, the distance fallen is
$h = \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2$, so

$$
t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}
$$

Scott dropped them from roughly $h = 1.6\ \text{m}$. On the Moon,
$g = 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2$:

$$
t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.6}{1.62}} \approx 1.4\ \text{s}
$$

On Earth ($g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$) with no air, the same drop takes about
$0.57\ \text{s}$, for the hammer and the feather alike.

**Where does $g$ come from?** Newton's law of gravitation says a planet of
mass $M$ pulls on an object of mass $m$, a distance $r$ from its centre, with
force $F = \dfrac{G M m}{r^2}$. Divide by $m$ and the object's mass cancels
again:

$$
g = \frac{G M}{r^2}
$$

$g$ is a property of the planet. For the Moon,
$M = 7.34 \times 10^{22}\ \text{kg}$ and $r = 1.74 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}$
give $g \approx 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2$, about a sixth of Earth's.

**Adding air.** Drag grows with the square of the speed $v$. Writing
$k = \tfrac{1}{2} \rho\, C_d A$ for the object's shape, size and the air's
density, the net force on a falling object is

$$
m a = m g - k v^2
$$

Now the mass does **not** cancel: $a = g - \dfrac{k}{m} v^2$. The object
stops accelerating when drag balances weight, at the _terminal velocity_

$$
v_t = \sqrt{\frac{m g}{k}}
$$

A small mass with a large $k$, like a feather, has a tiny terminal velocity.

<Callout kind="insight" title="A coincidence that isn't one">

The $m$ in $F = mg$ measures how strongly gravity grips an object. The $m$ in
$F = ma$ measures how much it resists being moved. Nothing in Newton's physics
says these must be the same number, yet experiments find them equal to about
one part in $10^{15}$. Einstein took that as a clue, and built general
relativity on it.

</Callout>

</Layer>

<Layer depth="build">

You can watch drag break the tie with a few lines of Python. The loop below
takes many small time steps. At each one it works out the acceleration from
the forces, then nudges the velocity and the height forward.

```python
def fall_time(m, k, h=1.6, g=9.81, dt=0.0001):
    """Seconds for an object to fall h metres. k is its drag constant."""
    y, v, t = h, 0.0, 0.0
    while y > 0:
        a = g - (k / m) * v**2   # gravity minus drag
        v += a * dt
        y -= v * dt
        t += dt
    return t

print(f"Hammer, no air:  {fall_time(1.32, 0):.2f} s")
print(f"Feather, no air: {fall_time(0.003, 0):.2f} s")
print(f"Hammer, in air:  {fall_time(1.32, 0.005):.2f} s")
print(f"Feather, in air: {fall_time(0.003, 0.01):.2f} s")
```

```text
Hammer, no air:  0.57 s
Feather, no air: 0.57 s
Hammer, in air:  0.57 s
Feather, in air: 1.05 s
```

With `k = 0` the mass makes no difference, just as the maths said. Turn the
air on and the hammer does not notice, while the 3-gram feather takes nearly twice
as long.
