<Step kind="see" title="Throw a ball straight up">

Throw a ball straight up and catch it again. Three questions describe its
flight at any moment: _where_ is it, _how fast_ is it moving, and how quickly
is that speed _changing_?

Each question has its own graph, and the three graphs are tied together by
derivatives. Throw the ball, then drag through time and watch all three at
once. On the top graph, the short amber line is the tangent.

<Interactive
  id="motion-graphs"
  title="One throw, three graphs"
  props={{ speed: 15, goal: "peak" }}
/>

Watch the tangent on the height graph as you drag. Its slope starts steep and
positive (the ball is rising fast), flattens to zero at the top, then turns
negative (the ball is falling). That slope, at each moment, is exactly the
number on the second graph.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Velocity">

**Velocity** is the derivative of position with respect to time:

$$
v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt}
$$

Its sign gives the direction: positive means moving in the positive direction
(here, up), negative means the other way. **Speed** is the size of the
velocity, ignoring the sign.

</Callout>

The velocity graph is a straight line sloping downwards, and a straight line
has the same slope everywhere. So the velocity is changing at a constant rate,
which is what the third graph shows.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Acceleration">

**Acceleration** is the derivative of velocity with respect to time, which
makes it the _second_ derivative of position:

$$
a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2}
$$

</Callout>

For the ball, the rules from the last lesson give all three at once. Thrown
upwards at $15$ m/s from the ground, its height in metres after $t$ seconds is

$$
h(t) = 15t - 4.9t^2
$$

Differentiate once for the velocity, and once more for the acceleration:

$$
v(t) = 15 - 9.8t \qquad\qquad a(t) = -9.8
$$

That constant $-9.8$ m/s² is gravity. It is the same on the way up, at the
top, and on the way down.

<Callout kind="insight" title="Zero velocity is not zero acceleration">

At the top of its flight the ball is not moving: $v = 0$. But its velocity is
still _changing_, from just above zero to just below it. If the acceleration
were zero there too, the ball would simply hang in the air.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A particle that turns around">

A particle moves along a line. Its position in metres after $t$ seconds is
$s(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t$. When is it at rest, and when is it moving backwards?

Differentiate to get the velocity:

$$
v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9 = 3(t - 1)(t - 3)
$$

It is at rest when $v = 0$, at $t = 1$ and $t = 3$. Between those times one
bracket is positive and the other negative, so $v$ is negative: the particle
is moving backwards from $t = 1$ to $t = 3$, and forwards otherwise.

Differentiate again for the acceleration: $a(t) = 6t - 12$. At $t = 1$ that
is $-6$ m/s², so although the particle has stopped, it is about to move
backwards.

</WorkedExample>

</Step>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

You have just gone _down_ the ladder: from position, to velocity, to
acceleration. Physics usually hands you the ladder from the other end.

Newton's second law says the forces on an object decide its acceleration.
From there you have to climb back _up_: from acceleration to velocity, and
from velocity to position. Undoing a derivative is the job of integration, or,
when no formula will do, of stepping forwards a little at a time with Euler's
method. That is exactly what a flight simulator does.
