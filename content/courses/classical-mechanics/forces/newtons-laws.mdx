<Step kind="see" title="What does a push do?">

So far you have described motion without asking what causes it. Time to ask.

Here is a block on a sheet of ice so slippery that there is no friction at
all. You can push it with a steady force for a while and then let go. Before
you try: once you stop pushing, what do you expect the block to do?

Two things are worth noticing.

**After the push ends, the block does not slow down.** It carries on at
exactly the speed it had. On ice, nothing is acting along the surface to
change its motion, so its motion does not change. Now switch the surface to
carpet and push again: the block stops, but only because friction, another
force, is acting on it.

**During the push, the block speeds up at a steady rate**, and that rate
depends on two things. Double the force and the acceleration doubles. Double
the mass and the acceleration halves.

Isaac Newton turned those observations into three laws, published in 1687.
They are still how engineers design bridges, cars and spacecraft.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Newton's first law">

An object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at a constant
speed, unless a net force acts on it.

</Callout>

This is not what everyday life suggests. On Earth, things you stop pushing
come to a halt, so for two thousand years people believed that motion needs a
force to keep it going. It does not. What it needs is the _absence_ of
friction and drag. Forces do not cause motion: they cause **changes** in
motion.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Newton's second law">

The acceleration of an object is the net force on it divided by its mass:

$$
\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\,\vec{a}
$$

Force is measured in **newtons**: 1 N is the force that gives 1 kg an
acceleration of 1 m/s².

</Callout>

Three things are packed into that small equation.

- **Net** force. If several forces act, add them as vectors first. Only the
  total matters.
- **Mass** measures how hard it is to change an object's motion, its
  _inertia_. The same push does less to a loaded trolley than to an empty one.
- It is a **vector** equation. The acceleration points the same way as the
  net force, which is not always the way the object is moving. A braking car
  moves forwards while its acceleration points backwards.

<WorkedExample title="A car pulling away">

A 1200 kg car's engine drives it forwards with 3000 N. Friction and air
resistance together push back with 600 N. What is its acceleration?

Add the forces along the road, taking forwards as positive:

$$
F_{\text{net}} = 3000 - 600 = 2400 \text{ N}
$$

Then the second law:

$$
a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{2400}{1200} = 2 \text{ m/s}^2
$$

</WorkedExample>

The third law is about where forces come from. A force is always something
one object does _to another_, and it never happens in one direction only.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Newton's third law">

If object A pushes on object B, then B pushes back on A with a force of the
same size in the opposite direction.

</Callout>

Push on a wall and the wall pushes on your hand: that is why your hand feels
it. A rocket throws exhaust gas backwards, and the gas pushes the rocket
forwards. You walk by pushing the ground backwards with your foot, and it is
the ground's push on you that moves you forwards.

<Callout kind="warning" title="The two forces never cancel">

The pair in the third law act on **different objects**: one on A, one on B.
To find out what happens to an object, you only add up the forces acting _on
that object_. The partner forces belong to somebody else's diagram.

</Callout>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

The second law is a machine: put in the net force, and out comes the
acceleration. From the acceleration, the equations of motion (or Euler's
method) give you everything else. So the real work in mechanics is finding
the net force, and that starts with a careful drawing of every force acting
on the object. Those drawings are called free-body diagrams.
