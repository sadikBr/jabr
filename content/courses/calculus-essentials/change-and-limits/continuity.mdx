<Step kind="see" title="Drawing without lifting your pen">

Some graphs you can draw in one stroke. Others force you to lift your pen: to
hop over a gap, or to jump to a different height. The first kind are called
**continuous**, and limits let us say precisely what that means, one point at
a time.

In the last lesson you saw that a limit ignores the value at the point itself.
Continuity is what you get when the two agree: when the function arrives
exactly where it was heading.

marked point. Then close in, and see which of three tests it passes.

Only the first one passed all three. Each of the others broke in its own way:
no value at the point, a value in the wrong place, two sides that disagree, or
no limit at all.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Continuous at a point">

A function $f$ is **continuous at $a$** when all three of these hold:

1. $f(a)$ is defined
2. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists
3. they are equal

In one line:

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)
$$

A function is simply called **continuous** when this is true at every point
where it is defined.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="insight" title="For continuous functions, limits are easy">

Read that one line again. It says: to find the limit of a continuous
function, just put the number in.

Most functions you know are continuous wherever they are defined: polynomials,
$\sin x$, $\cos x$, $e^x$, $\sqrt{x}$, and anything built from them by adding,
multiplying, dividing or composing. The trouble spots are the places where a
formula stops making sense, such as a division by zero, and the joins in
functions defined piece by piece.

</Callout>

Continuity is a promise of "no surprises", and promises can be used. Here is
the most useful one.

<Callout kind="definition" title="The intermediate value theorem">

If $f$ is continuous on the interval from $a$ to $b$, then it takes **every**
value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ somewhere in that interval.

</Callout>

It sounds obvious, and for anything that changes continuously it is. If it was
12 °C at 8 a.m. and 20 °C at noon, then at some moment in between it was
exactly 15 °C. The temperature cannot get from 12 to 20 without passing
through everything on the way.

Its most practical use is for finding where a function is zero. If a
continuous function is negative at one point and positive at another, it must
cross zero somewhere between them.

<WorkedExample title="Trapping a root">

Where does $f(x) = x^3 - x - 2$ equal zero? There is no easy formula, but
the function is a polynomial, so it is continuous. Try a couple of values:

$$
f(1) = -2 \qquad f(2) = 4
$$

One is negative and one positive, so there is a root between $1$ and $2$. Now
test the midpoint:

$$
f(1.5) = -0.125
$$

Negative. So the sign change, and with it the root, is between $1.5$ and $2$.
Test the midpoint again:

$$
f(1.75) \approx 1.61
$$

Positive, so the root is between $1.5$ and $1.75$. Each test halves the
interval. Ten more and you would know the root to three decimal places:
$x \approx 1.521$.

This is called **bisection**. It is slow but it cannot fail, as long as the
function is continuous.

</WorkedExample>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

Bisection is your first **numerical method**: a recipe that gets as close to
an answer as you like, by repeating a simple step, when no formula is
available. The same spirit drives Euler's method later in this course, and
every simulation built on it.

Continuity also sets the stage for the derivative. To find a slope at a point
you zoom in until the curve looks straight, and a function with a jump or a
hole there never will. Every function that has a derivative at a point is
continuous at that point.
