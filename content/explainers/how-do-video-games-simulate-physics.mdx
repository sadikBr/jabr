<Layer depth="intuition">

A film is a strip of still pictures shown quickly, one after another. A game
is the same, with one big difference: nobody drew the pictures in advance.
Sixty times a second the game has to work out, from scratch, where everything
is _now_.

It does that with the simplest rule imaginable. For every moving thing: take
where it was, and move it a little in the direction it is going.

Here is a ball dropped onto a thin platform, with every frame shown as a dot.
Those dots are all the game ever knows. Drop the ball. Then see whether you
can break the game.

<Interactive id="game-loop" props={{ fps: 60, goal: "break" }} />

At 60 frames a second the dots are close together and the bounce looks right.
At 10, the ball is above the platform in one frame and below it in the next.
The game checks "is the ball touching the platform?" once per frame, the
answer is "no" both times, and the ball sails through solid matter.

If you have ever seen a game character fall through the floor, or a fast car
pass through a wall, this is what happened. It even has a name:
**tunnelling**.

<Callout kind="insight" title="A game never sees motion, only snapshots">

Real things move continuously. A simulation knows the world only at separate
instants, and has to guess what happened in between. Almost everything
interesting about game physics, good and bad, comes from that one fact.

</Callout>

</Layer>

<Layer depth="mechanism">

**The game loop.** Every game is built around a loop that runs once per
frame, for as long as the game is open:

1. **Read the input.** Which buttons are held down?
2. **Update the world.** Move everything forwards by one small step of time.
3. **Draw the picture.**

Step 2 is the physics. For each object the game stores a **position** and a
**velocity**, and on every frame it does three things:

1. Add up the **forces** on the object (gravity, a jump, a push from an
   explosion) and divide by its mass to get its **acceleration**.
2. Change the **velocity** by acceleration × time step.
3. Change the **position** by velocity × time step.

Then it looks for **collisions**. If two things overlap, they are pushed
apart and their velocities are changed so that they bounce or stop.

That is the whole engine. A falling crate, a thrown grenade and a car
suspension are all this same loop with different forces.

<Question id="games.step-distance" />

**Curing tunnelling.** The cheap fix is more frames: halve the time step and
everything moves half as far each frame. But a fast enough object beats any
frame rate. The proper fix is to stop asking "where is it now?" and ask
"where has it _been_ since the last frame?" The game checks the whole line
from the old position to the new one against the scenery. This is called
**swept**, or **continuous**, collision detection. It costs more computing,
so games use it only for things that are small and fast.

Try it: the game below runs at a low frame rate. Make the ball bounce
properly without raising it above 20.

**Why the time step is fixed.** Your screen might refresh 60 times a second
and your friend's 144. If the physics simply used "the time since the last
picture", you and your friend would get slightly different jumps and
different bounces, and the same replay would play out differently on two
machines. So most games run their physics on its own steady clock, typically
50 or 60 steps a second, whatever the screen is doing, and draw whenever a
picture is needed.

</Layer>

<Layer depth="maths">

The update rule is **Euler's method**. Velocity is the derivative of
position, $v = \frac{dx}{dt}$, and acceleration is the derivative of
velocity, $a = \frac{dv}{dt}$. Over a short step $\Delta t$, a derivative says
how much its quantity changes:

$$
v_{\text{new}} = v + a\,\Delta t \qquad\qquad x_{\text{new}} = x + v\,\Delta t
$$

Each step pretends the velocity and acceleration stay constant for the whole
of $\Delta t$. They do not quite, so each step is slightly wrong, by an amount
proportional to $\Delta t^2$. Over a second there are $1/\Delta t$ steps, so
the errors add up to something proportional to $\Delta t$. Halve the time
step and the simulation is about twice as accurate, at twice the cost.

**The order of the two lines matters.** There is a choice hidden in the rule
above: when updating the position, do you use the _old_ velocity or the _new_
one?

$$
\text{old velocity:}\quad x_{\text{new}} = x + v\,\Delta t
\qquad\qquad
\text{new velocity:}\quad x_{\text{new}} = x + v_{\text{new}}\,\Delta t
$$

It looks like a detail. It is not. Take a mass on a spring, which ought to
swing back and forth for ever with the same energy. With the old-velocity
version, every single step multiplies the energy by $1 + \omega^2 \Delta t^2$,
where $\omega$ sets how fast the spring swings. That is only a fraction of a
percent, but it compounds. For a spring with $\omega = 4$ per second at 60
steps a second:

$$
\left(1 + \frac{4^2}{60^2}\right)^{600} \approx 14
$$

After ten seconds the spring has fourteen times the energy it started with,
out of nowhere. In a game, that is a rope bridge that shakes itself to pieces.

The new-velocity version, called **semi-implicit Euler**, makes errors too,
but they do not pile up: the energy wobbles by a few percent and stays put.
It costs nothing extra, just the two lines in the other order. This is why
game engines update velocity first.
