<Step kind="see" title="Trace a derivative, spot a pattern">

You found the derivative of $x^2$ from the definition: expand, cancel, take
the limit. It works, but doing that for every function would be slow.
Fortunately, derivatives follow patterns.

Remember that a derivative is itself a function: it gives the slope at
_every_ point. So you can draw it. Slide the point along the curve below and
the slope at each position is plotted underneath. Sweep the whole curve to
trace out its derivative, then try the other functions.

<Interactive id="derivative-explorer" title="Draw the derivative" />

The slopes of $x^2$ traced a straight line, $2x$. The slopes of $x^3$ traced a
parabola, $3x^2$. Put them in a row, along with a couple more that the
definition gives:

| Function   | $x^2$ | $x^3$  | $x^4$  | $x^5$  |
| ---------- | ----- | ------ | ------ | ------ |
| Derivative | $2x$  | $3x^2$ | $4x^3$ | $5x^4$ |

Each time, the power comes down in front and the power itself drops by one.

<Callout kind="definition" title="The power rule">

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\, x^n = n\,x^{n-1}
$$

It works for every power, not just whole numbers. Since $\frac{1}{x} = x^{-1}$
and $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{1}{x} = -x^{-2} = -\frac{1}{x^2}
\qquad\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\,\sqrt{x} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}
$$

</Callout>

The notation $\frac{d}{dx}$ just means "the derivative of what follows, with
respect to $x$". It says the same thing as the dash in $f'(x)$.

<Question id="calc.rules.power-slope" />

Most functions are built from several pieces. Two facts deal with the
simplest ways of combining them, and both are what you would hope.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Constants and sums">

A constant multiple stays where it is, and a sum is differentiated one term
at a time:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\,\big[c\,f(x)\big] = c\,f'(x)
\qquad\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\,\big[f(x) + g(x)\big] = f'(x) + g'(x)
$$

A constant on its own has derivative $0$: its graph is flat.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A whole polynomial in one line">

To differentiate $3x^4 - 5x + 7$, take the terms one at a time.

- $3x^4$ becomes $3 \cdot 4x^3 = 12x^3$
- $-5x$ becomes $-5$, because $x = x^1$ has derivative $1 \cdot x^0 = 1$
- $7$ becomes $0$

So

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\,\big(3x^4 - 5x + 7\big) = 12x^3 - 5
$$

</WorkedExample>

<Step kind="see" title="Products and chains">

Sums were easy. Products are where intuition goes wrong.

It is tempting to guess that the derivative of a product is the product of the
derivatives. Test the guess on $x \cdot x$. Each factor has derivative $1$, so
the guess gives $1 \cdot 1 = 1$. But $x \cdot x = x^2$, whose derivative is
$2x$. The guess is wrong.

Here is what really happens. Think of $f \cdot g$ as the area of a rectangle
with sides $f$ and $g$. When $x$ changes a little, both sides grow. The area
gains a strip along one side, of size (change in $f$) $\times\, g$, and a
strip along the other, of size $f \times$ (change in $g$). There is also a
tiny corner piece, but it is a small change times a small change, and it
vanishes in the limit.

<Callout kind="definition" title="The product rule">

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\,\big[f(x)\,g(x)\big] = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x)
$$

Differentiate each factor in turn, leaving the other alone, and add.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Checking the product rule against the power rule">

Take $x^2 \cdot x^3$. With $f = x^2$ and $g = x^3$:

$$
f'g + fg' = 2x \cdot x^3 + x^2 \cdot 3x^2 = 2x^4 + 3x^4 = 5x^4
$$

And since $x^2 \cdot x^3 = x^5$, the power rule gives $5x^4$ directly. They
agree, as they must.

</WorkedExample>

The explorer above showed one more derivative worth remembering, and two
relatives come with it:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\,\sin x = \cos x
\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\,\cos x = -\sin x
\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\,e^x = e^x
$$

With those you can try a product that cannot be multiplied out.

<Question id="calc.rules.product" />

The last rule handles a function _inside_ another function, like
$(3x + 1)^4$ or $\sin(x^2)$. The idea behind it is that **rates multiply**.

Picture three gears. If the second turns twice as fast as the first, and the
third turns three times as fast as the second, then the third turns
$2 \times 3 = 6$ times as fast as the first.

<Question id="calc.rules.rates-multiply" />

<Callout kind="definition" title="The chain rule">

If $y$ depends on $u$, and $u$ depends on $x$, then

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}
$$

In function notation: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\, f\big(g(x)\big) = f'\big(g(x)\big) \cdot g'(x)$.

Differentiate the outside, leaving the inside alone, then multiply by the
derivative of the inside.

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A function inside a function">

Take $y = (3x + 1)^2$. The inside is $u = 3x + 1$ and the outside is $u^2$.

- Outside: $u^2$ has derivative $2u = 2(3x + 1)$
- Inside: $3x + 1$ has derivative $3$

Multiply:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = 2(3x + 1) \cdot 3 = 6(3x + 1) = 18x + 6
$$

You can check this one by multiplying out first: $(3x + 1)^2 = 9x^2 + 6x + 1$,
which has derivative $18x + 6$.

</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="warning" title="The mistake everyone makes once">

Forgetting to multiply by the derivative of the inside. The derivative of
$(3x + 1)^2$ is **not** $2(3x + 1)$. The inside is changing three times as
fast as $x$, and the answer has to say so.

</Callout>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

With these four rules and a short list of known derivatives, you can
differentiate any formula built from powers, sines, cosines and exponentials.
No limits needed. That is what makes calculus practical.

The first place to use them is motion. If you have a formula for where
something is, one derivative tells you how fast it is moving, and a second
tells you how quickly that is changing.
