<Step kind="see" title="Getting close without arriving">

Here is a function with a problem:

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}
$$

Put in $x = 1$ and you get $\frac{0}{0}$, which is not a number. The function
simply has no value there.

But nothing stops you asking about $x = 1.1$, or $x = 0.99$, or $x = 1.0001$.
So ask. Close in on $x = 1$ from both sides and watch the values.

<Interactive id="limit-explorer" props={{ cases: ["hole"] }} />

From the left the values run $1.9$, $1.99$, $1.999$. From the right they run
$2.1$, $2.01$, $2.001$. Both sides are closing in on $2$, and you can get as
close to $2$ as you like by taking $x$ close enough to $1$.

That target has a name: it is the **limit** of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $1$.

<Callout kind="definition" title="The limit">

We write

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L
$$

and say "the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ is $L$" when $f(x)$ gets as
close to $L$ as we like, provided $x$ is close enough to $a$ **without being
equal to $a$**.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="insight" title="The limit never looks at the point itself">

A limit is about the journey, not the destination. What $f$ does _at_ $a$ is
irrelevant: it might have no value there, or a value somewhere unexpected.
The limit only cares about the values nearby.

</Callout>

Sliding closer and closer is convincing, but algebra can settle it for good.

<WorkedExample title="Why the limit is exactly 2">

The top of the fraction factorises:

$$
\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1}
$$

For any $x$ other than $1$, the factor $x - 1$ is not zero and cancels,
leaving

$$
f(x) = x + 1 \qquad (x \neq 1)
$$

So away from $x = 1$ this function _is_ the straight line $x + 1$, with a
single point missing. As $x$ approaches $1$, the line approaches $1 + 1 = 2$:

$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = 2
$$

We never put $x = 1$ into the original fraction. We did not need to.

</WorkedExample>

Not every journey has a destination. Try these three, and for each one decide
what goes wrong before you read the verdict.

<Interactive

When the two sides disagree it helps to talk about them separately. The
**left-hand limit** $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ uses only values of $x$ below $a$,
and the **right-hand limit** $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ only values above. The
limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.

</Step>

<Step kind="connect" title="Where this goes next">

Look back at the ramp from the last lesson. The average speed between $t = 2$
and $t = 2 + h$ was

$$
\frac{(2 + h)^2 - 4}{h}
$$

At $h = 0$ that is $\frac{0}{0}$: the same trouble as the function on this
page. But now you know the right question to ask. Not "what is it _at_
$h = 0$?" but "what is its limit as $h$ approaches $0$?" The answer, $4$, is
the ball's speed at the instant $t = 2$.

A limit of average rates of change is called a **derivative**, and it is the
central idea of this course. Before that, one short stop: the functions where
limits hold no surprises at all are called **continuous**.
